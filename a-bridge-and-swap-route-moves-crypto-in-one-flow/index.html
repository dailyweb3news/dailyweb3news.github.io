A bridge-and-swap route moves crypto in one flow

A bridge-and-swap route moves value to another blockchain and trades it into a chosen token, combining two steps while adding fees, timing, and trust choices.

The Onchain Brief EditorsOct 16, 2026 · 11:49 UTC3 min read

A bridge-and-swap route moves crypto from one blockchain to another and swaps it into a chosen token as part of one flow. It combines a bridge, which transfers value between chains, with a swap, which trades one token for another. For example, you might start with a stablecoin on one chain and end with a different token on another. The route can save you from arranging each step yourself.

How does a bridge-and-swap route work?

The route starts with your input token, the chain it is on, and the token and chain you want to receive. A route finder checks whether it can bridge your starting asset and swap it for the destination asset. For a closer look at how those choices work for Mantle, see this guide to the mantle bridge. The exact steps depend on the service and the assets involved.

Often, the service sends the input token across first, then swaps it on the destination chain. Some routes use a third party, such as a solver: a service that supplies the destination funds and later claims the matching payment on the source chain. In either case, the two blockchains do not share one transaction record. Each chain records its own part, and the route depends on the bridge mechanism to connect them.

The screen may show an estimated amount, fees, and time before you confirm. The estimate can change before completion if prices or available liquidity shift. Liquidity means the tokens available to fill a trade. You may also need a small amount of the destination chain’s native token to pay for later transactions there.

What does one route combine?

A simple trip from Token A on Chain One to Token B on Chain Two might involve a swap, a bridge transfer, and another swap. A combined route presents these as one request. Behind the scenes, it may choose an intermediate token or split the trade across available pools. The chosen path can affect your final amount and how long you wait.

Compare the full amount you are expected to receive, not just the headline fee. Check these details before signing:

  • The source and destination chains.
  • The exact token you will receive and its contract address.
  • Estimated fees, including network fees on both chains.
  • The minimum amount the route guarantees, if one is shown.

What are the trade-offs?

A combined route is convenient, but it adds moving parts. The bridge and swap can each have separate fees, delays, and security assumptions. A route using a third party also depends on that party delivering the destination funds as promised. If a step stalls, the service may show a pending transfer or provide instructions for recovery; the outcome depends on its design.

For a small transfer, one flow may be easier than managing several transactions. For a large transfer, compare the route’s expected receipt and trust model with doing the bridge and swap separately. Read the transaction details before signing, and do not approve a token allowance larger than you intend to spend when the wallet lets you set a limit.
