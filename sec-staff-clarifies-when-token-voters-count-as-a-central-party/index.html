SEC staff clarifies when token voters count as a central party

SEC staff added answers on Nov. 2 explaining when token voters may count as a group controlling a network, and when broad governance may not.

The Onchain Brief EditorsNov 9, 2026 · 02:06 UTC3 min read

The SEC’s Corporation Finance staff added answers on Nov. 2 explaining when participants in a crypto network’s governance may count as a group controlling it. The answers help clarify how staff views decentralization when assessing whether a crypto asset is a digital commodity, according to the SEC’s updated crypto asset FAQs.

The new answers address who qualifies as a “central party” under the SEC’s March interpretation of federal securities laws for certain crypto assets. The FAQs are staff views, not a Commission rule or statement. The SEC says they have no legal force and create no new obligations.

When can token voters count as a controlling group?

Governance participants generally will not count as a group if they are not affiliated and have no formal or informal agreement to act together outside their participation in the governance system, the staff said. That answer also applies when governance sits within an organization, association or foundation and participation depends on holding the system’s native token.

The staff added that even if participants do count as a group, they must also have operational, economic or voting control of the system to meet the SEC’s definition of a central party. A “broad-based governance system” means a transparent, rules-based process in which diverse stakeholders can contribute and reach agreement. The staff says decision-making power should not be concentrated in one person or a coordinated group.

What actions amount to overseeing participation?

A central party oversees participation if it can control access at the protocol, validator or user level. The staff listed unilateral powers such as deciding who can validate transactions, changing voting processes, modifying the system’s code, reversing transactions or revoking access.

By contrast, the staff said a party generally does not oversee participation when the system runs automatically under rules-based code. The same can apply when discretionary decisions are made solely through broad-based governance, as long as those participants do not themselves qualify as a central party.

How does decentralization differ from functionality?

The staff described functionality and decentralization as separate ideas. A system is functional when its native asset can be used according to the system’s programmed purpose. It is decentralized when it operates autonomously and no person or group has operational, economic or voting control.

The FAQs say a functional system can have varying degrees of decentralization. The staff’s answers clarify its view under the March interpretation; they do not settle how the law applies to any particular network. The SEC also cautioned that the governance answer applies only to the interpretation’s description of digital commodities, not to other definitions under securities laws.
