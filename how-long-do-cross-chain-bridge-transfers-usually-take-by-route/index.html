How Long Do Cross-Chain Bridge Transfers Usually Take? By Route

Cross-chain bridge transfers usually take minutes to an hour; Bitcoin confirmations add time, while native optimistic rollup withdrawals to Ethereum take about seven days.

The Onchain Brief EditorsNov 25, 2026 · 22:37 UTC3 min read

Most cross-chain bridge transfers take minutes to about an hour. Native optimistic rollup withdrawals to Ethereum are the exception: they take about seven days.

A transfer has three main stages: the source chain confirms your deposit, the bridge verifies it, then a transaction delivers the asset on the destination chain. A quick estimate is source confirmations multiplied by block time, plus bridge verification and the destination transaction. The source chain’s confirmation rules set much of the wait.

For RenBridge users, a Ledger affects how they approve the Bitcoin deposit and later Ethereum mint. The piece on how RenBridge fit Ledger wallets explains which signing steps the wallet handles, helping you separate approval time from the bridge’s wait.

Why do bridge routes take different amounts of time?

Bridge type and direction decide which stages take longest. A lock-and-mint bridge waits for the source deposit to be verified before creating a wrapped asset on the destination chain. A liquidity bridge can pay from a pool within minutes, while the source transfer settles separately; the faster payout comes with a fee.

Rollups have a sharp difference by direction. A deposit from Ethereum to an optimistic rollup usually lands in minutes. A native withdrawal back to Ethereum waits through a roughly seven-day challenge period, which gives time to dispute a withdrawal. Liquidity bridges can avoid that wait by paying from a pool, but charge for the service.

Bitcoin routes can also take longer because Bitcoin blocks average about 10 minutes. A low transaction fee or network congestion can delay the first confirmation, and every required confirmation adds more time before the bridge can act.

What happened during a RenBridge Bitcoin transfer?

RenBridge’s Bitcoin-to-Ethereum route shows how those stages stack up. A user sent BTC to a deposit address. The bridge waited for the required Bitcoin confirmations, then Darknodes—operators that helped verify and process the transfer—signed it. The user then submitted an Ethereum transaction to mint renBTC, a Bitcoin-backed token on Ethereum.

So the total wait included Bitcoin blocks, bridge verification and the user’s mint transaction. The confirmation requirement determined how many roughly 10-minute blocks came first; signing and the Ethereum transaction followed. The exact total depended on those steps and how quickly the user submitted the mint.

This distinction helps when estimating a return trip too: an Ethereum-to-Bitcoin lock-and-mint transfer follows its own source-chain confirmation rules. A bridge’s name alone does not tell you the wait. Check the route, direction and whether the bridge uses a pool or waits for verification.

Why is my bridge transfer stuck on pending?

A pending transfer may still be waiting for source-chain confirmations. A low fee or congestion can delay the first one, so the bridge may not yet be able to verify the deposit.

Can I speed up a bridge transfer after sending?

Once sent, you generally have to wait for the route’s required confirmations and verification. For next time, choose a suitable source-chain fee and check whether a liquidity route’s fee is worth a faster payout; this avoids mistaking a slow first confirmation for a failed bridge.
