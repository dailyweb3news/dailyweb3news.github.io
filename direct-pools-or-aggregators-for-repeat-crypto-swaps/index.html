Direct Pools or Aggregators for Repeat Crypto Swaps?

A direct pool can suit repeat swaps when its liquidity is deep, while an aggregator can find a better route across pools; compare net output each time.

The Onchain Brief EditorsOct 23, 2026 · 15:19 UTC3 min read

For repeat swaps, a direct pool is often simpler when it has enough liquidity, while an aggregator can help when liquidity is spread across pools. The better choice depends on how much you swap, the pool’s price, and the costs of the route. A route is the path a trade takes through one or more pools.

A direct swap sends tokens through one pool for that pair. An aggregator checks several pools and may route a trade through more than one to find a better exchange rate. For the wallet steps behind a swap, this SpookySwap guide to swapping from your own wallet covers the process in more detail. The choice of route still comes down to the amount you trade and the pools available at that moment.

What is the difference between a direct pool and an aggregator?

A direct pool uses one market, while an aggregator searches across available markets and routes. In a pool, traders swap against tokens held by other users. The pool’s price shifts as its token balance changes, so a large trade can get a worse rate than a small one. This is called price impact.

An aggregator can split a trade or send it through multiple pools. That can improve the rate when no single pool can fill the trade well. But extra steps can mean more network fees, and each pool in the route may charge a trading fee. A quoted rate alone does not show the whole cost.

Which route works better for repeat swaps?

A direct pool often makes sense for frequent swaps of the same pair if it is deep enough to handle your usual trade without much price impact. Fewer steps can make the route easier to check. An aggregator is more useful when liquidity for the pair is scattered or when a larger swap would move one pool’s price sharply.

Do not assume that a route that worked yesterday will still be best today. Pool prices and balances change with trading. For each swap, compare:

  • The amount of the token you will receive after fees.
  • The network fee for submitting the swap.
  • How many pools the route uses.

For repeat trades, make the comparison using the same input amount and token pair. Otherwise, the quotes may not be comparable. A route with a slightly better exchange rate may cost more to execute if it uses extra steps.

How can you compare swap quotes?

Compare the estimated amount received after fees and network costs, then check the route before signing. Look at the token names and addresses, the input and output amounts, and the slippage setting. Slippage is the amount the final price can move before the swap fails. A very loose setting can let a trade complete at a worse price than expected.

For a familiar pair with a deep pool, a direct swap is a good starting point. Check an aggregator quote when the trade is large or the direct pool’s price looks poor. The useful habit is to compare the final cost each time, rather than sticking to one route by default.
