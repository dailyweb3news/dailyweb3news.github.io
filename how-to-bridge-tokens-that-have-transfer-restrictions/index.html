How to Bridge Tokens That Have Transfer Restrictions

A restricted token can cross chains only if its rules allow the bridge and destination wallet; check both sides and confirm how tokens are issued.

The Onchain Brief EditorsOct 24, 2026 · 11:25 UTC3 min read

To bridge a token with transfer restrictions, first check that its rules permit the bridge contract to move it and the destination wallet to receive it. A bridge usually locks tokens on one chain and releases or creates a matching token on another. A rule that blocks either step can stop the transfer, even when the bridge lists the asset.

How do transfer restrictions affect a bridge?

A restricted token’s contract checks who can send or receive it. It may use a blocklist, an allowlist, a freeze, or a global pause. An allowlist means only approved wallets can transact. Some contracts check the sender and receiver on every transfer, including transfers made by a bridge.

So the bridge contract may need approval to take custody, and your destination wallet may need approval to receive the token there. A bridge can also use a separate token contract on the destination chain. The Mantle Bridge guide covers a routine transfer flow in more detail; the same checks matter when an asset has extra rules.

What should you check before sending?

Check both the token and the bridge on the source and destination chains. The same ticker or token name does not prove that two contracts are the same asset or follow the same restrictions. Look for the official token addresses and the bridge’s supported route, then confirm the issuer’s rules for that route.

  • Can your wallet send the token to the bridge contract?
  • Is the bridge contract approved to hold or burn the source token?
  • Can the destination token be issued to your wallet, and can your wallet receive it?
  • Does the token issuer require an approved bridge or a separate transfer process?

If the bridge offers a transaction preview, use it to catch an obvious contract rejection before signing. A preview cannot guarantee that every later step will succeed, especially if the token’s rules or your wallet’s status changes while the transfer is pending.

What happens if a transfer is blocked?

A failed source transaction usually leaves the tokens in your wallet, though you may still pay a network fee. If the source transfer succeeds but the destination step fails, the outcome depends on the bridge’s design and recovery process. Check its status page or support instructions before sending again; a second transfer could create a separate transaction.

Some bridges lock the original token and issue a wrapped version, which is a token meant to represent it on another chain. That version may have different controls. The source token’s restrictions do not automatically carry over unless the destination contract and bridge are designed to enforce them.

For most holders, the better choice is a route explicitly supported by the token issuer and bridge. If neither confirms the route, do not assume a small test will make an unsupported transfer safe.
